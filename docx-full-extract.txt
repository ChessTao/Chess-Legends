William Steinitz (1836-1900) was an Austrian and, later, an American master, the first World Chess Champion (1886-1894). 
Main chess achievements.
He won the title of the first World Chess Champion in the match versus  Johannes Zukertort held in New York, Saint Louis, and New Orleans in 1886 (12.5-7.5). Later, he successfully defended his title three times - against Mikhail Chigorin in 1889 (10.5-6.5), against Isidor Gunsberg in 1890 (10.5-8.5), and again versus Mikhail Chigorin in 1892 (12.5-10.5).
Steinitz lost the title to Emanuel Lasker in 1894 (12-7) and lost a rematch in 1896–97 (12.5-4.5).
Steinitz was unbeaten in match play for 32 years, from 1862 to 1894. From 1873-1882, Steinitz went on a historic 25-game winning streak. 
Legacy.
In the early 1870s, Steinitz introduced a new style of play and is subsequently regarded as the father of positional chess. He displayed many positional elements of the game that are still recognized today: the importance of a sound pawn structure, space, the bishop pair, creating and utilizing outposts for knights, and the accumulation of small advantages.
Steinitz was an active chess writer. He was the principal chess correspondent of The Field (in London) from 1873 to 1882. In 1885, he founded the International Chess Magazine in New York City and edited it until 1891. In addition to game commentaries of the first World Championship match with Johann Zukertort and the 1889 American Chess Congress, he wrote several articles about Paul Morphy. He wrote the book of the 1889 New York tournament, and also in 1889, he published a textbook, The Modern Chess Instructor, that is still available in the bookshops.

Emanuel Lasker (1868-1941) was a German chess player, mathematician, and philosopher, the second World Chess Champion (1894-1921).
Main chess achievements.
His results in the World Championship matches are as follows: 12-7 and 12.5-4.5 against William Steinitz in 1894 and 1896 correspondingly; 11.5-3.5 against Frank Marshall in 1907; 10.5-5.5 against Siegbert Tarrasch in 1908; 8-2 against Dawid Janowski in 1909; 5-5 against Carl Schlechter in 1910; 9.5-1.5 against Dawid Janowski in 1910. He lost the title in 1921 to Jose Raul Capablanca with a score of 5-9. 
In 1935, at age 66, Lasker finished third in the most robust international tournament in Moscow. In  this way, he was an elite player for about five decades, which is an incredible achievement!
Legacy.
Lasker’s reign is the longest (27 years!) of any officially recognized World Chess Champion in history.
Lasker is widely known for his contributions to many openings, such as Ruy Lopez, Queen's Gambit, French Defense, and Sicilian Defense. 
Lasker was also a chess author - he published chess magazines most of his life and wrote five chess books. His Manual of Chess can still be found in chess bookstores.
Lasker had earned a Ph.D. in Mathematics with his 100-page 1905 thesis “Zur Theorie der Moduln und Ideale.” This work is regarded as one of the foundations of modern algebra.
Albert Einstein (according to Wikipedia, “one of the greatest and most influential scientists of all time”) was a good friend of Lasker's. In the introduction to Lasker's biography, Einstein wrote: “Emanuel Lasker was undoubtedly one of the most interesting people I came to know in my later life. Few indeed can have combined such a unique independence of personality with so eager an interest in all the great problems of mankind.”

Jose Raul Capablanca (1888-1942) was a Cuban chess player, the third World Chess Champion (1921-1927).
Main chess achievements.
Capablanca won the World Chess Champion title in 1921 in Havana by beating Emanuel Lasker with a score of 9-5. From 1916 to 1924, Capablanca did not lose a single tournament game.
In 1927, Capablanca lost the world champion title to Alexander Alekhine. After that, he kept playing well in the tournaments. The last significant achievement of the Great Cuban was a win (tied with Mikhail Botvinnik) in Nottingham-1936, ahead of the strongest players, including Emanuel Lasker and Alexander Alekhine.
Legacy.
He wrote several chess books, of which Chess Fundamentals is a classic introductory book for beginners and intermediate players.
Most of the “best players of all time” lists put Capablanca at least in the top-5. Capablanca heavily influenced many world champions. One of the best quotes highlighting Capablanca's place in history belongs to Emanuel Lasker, who said: "I have known many chess players, but only one chess genius: Capablanca."

Alexander Alekhine (1892-1946) was a Russian and French chess player, the fourth World Chess Champion (1927-1935, 1937-1946).
Main chess achievements.
In 1914, Alekhine took third place behind Emanuel Lasker and Jose Raul Capablanca in the St. Petersburg 1914 chess tournament. From 1921 to 1927, Alekhine won or shared first prize in about two-thirds of the many tournaments he played. In November 1921, Alekhine challenged the world champion Jose Raul Capablanca for a title match but needed help raising $10,000. To collect money for the game, Alekhine went on tour, playing simultaneous exhibitions for modest fees day after day.
In New York on April 27, 1924, he broke the world record for simultaneous blindfold play when he played twenty-six opponents, winning sixteen games, drawing five, and losing five after twelve hours of play. He broke his world record on February 1, 1925, by playing twenty-eight games blindfold simultaneously in Paris, winning twenty-two, drawing three, and losing three.
In 1927, a group of Argentine businessmen and the president of Argentina guaranteed the funds for the world championship match. Alekhine became the fourth World Chess Champion by beating Capablanca with a score of 18.5-15.5.
Alekhine successfully defended the title in his matches against Efim Bogoljubow: 15.5-9.5 in 1929 and 15.5-10.5 in 1934. But the next title defense could have been better. Alekhine lost to Max Euwe in the Netherlands in 1935, scoring 14.5-15.5. Two years later, these players played a rematch, in which Alekhine took the title back (15.5-9.5).
Alekhine is the only world champion to die while holding the title. 
Legacy.
Alekhine is known for his fierce and imaginative attacking style and great positional and endgame skills. To demonstrate his influence on modern chess, Garry Kasparov said: "Alexander Alekhine is the first luminary among the others who are still having the greatest influence on me. I like his universality, his approach to the game, and his chess ideas. I am sure that the future belongs to Alekhine chess."
He is highly regarded as a chess writer and theoretician. Several openings and opening variations are named after Alekhine.

Max Euwe (1901-1981) was a Dutch chess player and mathematician, the fifth World Chess Champion (1935-1937), and FIDE president (1970-1978).
Main chess achievements.
His 12 Dutch chess champion titles are still a record. In 1928, Euwe became the World Amateur Chess Champion.
A few matches were organized in the Netherlands against the strongest players of those years to prepare for the world championship match. Though Euwe’s results in these competitions were not outstanding, he won the title match against Alexander Alekhine in 1935, scoring 15.5-14.5, and became the fifth World Chess Champion. In 1937, he lost a rematch (15.5-9.5). 
He participated for the Netherlands in seven Chess Olympiads from 1927-62, always on the first board. Euwe won a total of 102 first prizes in tournaments during his career.
Legacy.
Euwe wrote over 70 chess books; some of the best-known are The Road to Chess Mastery, Judgement and Planning in Chess, The Logical Approach to Chess, and Strategy and Tactics in Chess.
From 1970 until 1978, Euwe was president of FIDE (International Chess Federation). He is considered one of the best presidents FIDE has ever had and the only person in history to hold both World Champion and FIDE President titles.
Max Euwe is a national hero in the Netherlands. In Amsterdam, there is an area known as Max Euweplein, which contains his sculpture.

Mikhail Botvinnik (1911-1995) was a Soviet grandmaster, the sixth World Chess Champion (1948-1957; 1958-1960; 1961-1963). 
Main chess achievements.
In 1925, at the age of 14, he defeated world champion Jose Raul Capablanca in a simultaneous exhibition game. In 1931, he won his first Soviet Championship. Overall, he won a record six USSR champion titles.
Botvinnik placed first equal with Flohr, a half point ahead of the second world champion Emanuel Lasker and one point ahead of the third world champion Jose Raul Capablanca, in Moscow's second international tournament, held in 1935.
In 1948, the world championship tournament of the five best players of that time was held in two cities - the Hague and Moscow. Botvinnik finished first, 3 points ahead of the field, and crowned the sixth World Chess Champion.
Botvinnik defended his world champion title twice with an equal score of 12-12 - in 1951 by drawing the match with David Bronstein and in 1954 with Vasily Smyslov. In 1957, he was defeated by Smyslov with a score of 12.5-9.5, and in 1958 Botvinnik reclaimed the title with a score 12.5-10.5. In 1960, he lost 12.5-8.5 to Mikhail Tal, but he again opted for a rematch and regained the title by scoring 13-8. In 1963, Botvinnik lost the world champion title to Tigran Petrosian 12.5-9.5.
Botvinnik scored a convincing 74.6 percent (54.5 out of 73) in six Chess Olympiads, helping the Soviet team win six gold and five individual medals. 
Legacy.
In 1963, Botvinnik founded his chess school, along with other Soviet trainers.  This school has produced three world champions (Karpov, Kasparov, and Kramnik) and many other strong grandmasters. 
His nickname was The Patriarch of the Soviet Chess School.

Vasily Smyslov (1921-2010) was a Soviet and Russian grandmaster, the seventh World Chess Champion (1957-1958).
Main chess achievements.
In the 1946 Groningen tournament, Smyslov started a series of high finishes against the strong opposition.  As a result, Smyslov was one of the five players selected to compete in the 1948 World Chess Championship tournament to determine the sixth world champion. Smyslov finished second behind Mikhail Botvinnik.
In the 1950 Budapest Candidates’ tournament, Smyslov finished third. After winning the Candidates’ Tournament in Zurich in 1953, Smyslov played a match with Botvinnik for the title the following year. The match ended in a draw after 24 games (seven wins each and ten draws), meaning that Botvinnik retained his title.
Smyslov again won the Candidates’ Tournament in Amsterdam in 1956. His second attempt was successful - he won the match against Mikhail Botvinnik with a score of 12.5–9.5 and became the seventh World Chess Champion. Botvinnik exercised his right to a rematch the following year, winning the title back. 
Smyslov did not play another world championship match but continued to play in world championship cycles. In 1983, at 62, he went to the Candidates’ final (the match to determine who plays the champion), losing 8.5–4.5 at Vilnius in 1984 to Garry Kasparov, who was only 21.
Smyslov represented the Soviet Union nine times at Chess Olympiads from 1952 to 1972. His total of 17 Olympiad medals, including eight team and seven individual, is an all-time Olympiad record. 
Smyslov also represented the USSR in five European Team Championships and emerged with a perfect medals record: he won five team gold medals and five board gold medals.
In 1991, Smyslov won the inaugural World Senior Chess Championship.
Legacy.
He made enormous contributions to chess opening theory - many variations are named after him.
Smyslov wrote a few chess books about his games and a work about the rook endgames (together with Grigory Levenfish).
Smyslov was a baritone singer and only decided upon a chess career after a failed audition with the Bolshoi Theatre in 1950. He recorded several arias during his life.

Mikhail Tal (1936-1992) was a Latvian and Soviet chess player, the eighth World Chess Champion (1960-1961). 
Main chess achievements.
In 1957, Tal became the youngest player to win the USSR Championship. In 1958, he won the Interzonal tournament at Portoroz. After that, Tal showed superior form by winning the Candidates’ Tournament in Yugoslavia in 1959, scoring 20 points in 28 games.
In 1960, Tal defeated Mikhail Botvinnik in a world championship match held in Moscow with a score of 12.5-8.5 and became the eighth World Chess Champion. Botvinnik won the rematch in 1961.
Tal played in six Candidates’ Tournaments and match cycles, though he never again earned the right to play for the world title.
From July 1972 to April 1973, Tal played 86 consecutive games without a loss (47 wins and 39 draws). Between October 1973 and October 1974, he played 95 straight games without a loss (46 wins and 49 draws), shattering his previous record. These were the two longest unbeaten streaks in competitive chess for over four decades until Ding Liren broke the record in 2018 with 100 games (29 wins, 71 draws).
Tal played in 21 Soviet Championships, winning a record six times (1957, 1958, 1967, 1972, 1974, 1978).
In Olympiad play, Mikhail Tal was a member of eight Soviet teams, each time winning a gold medal, scoring 81.2 percent in his games. This percentage makes him the player with the best score among those participating in at least four Olympiads. Individually, Tal won seven Olympiad board medals, including five gold and two silver. Tal also represented the Soviet Union at six European Team Championships, winning gold medals each time.
His last significant achievement was winning the World Blitz Championship in 1988. 
Legacy.
His attacking and daring combinatorial style earned him the nickname "The Magician from Riga."
The Tal Memorial elite chess tournaments have been held in Moscow annually from 2006 until 2018.

Tigran Petrosian (1929-1984) was a Soviet-Armenian chess player, the ninth World Chess Champion (1963-1969).
Main chess achievements.
In 1959, Petrosian won his first USSR Championship. Having won the Candidates’ tournament (Curacao, 1962), Petrosian earned the right to challenge the reigning world champion Mikhail Botvinnik for the title match. He won the match with a final score of 12.5-9.5 and became the ninth World Chess Champion.
In 1966, Petrosian was challenged for the title match by Boris Spassky, who won the Candidates’ cycle. Petrosian defended his title by scoring 12.5-11.5. In 1969, Spassky again became the challenger. This time, Petrosian lost with a score of 12.5-10.5.
Petrosian was the defending World Champion or a World Championship Candidate in ten consecutive three-year cycles. He won four USSR championships.
Petrosian made ten straight Soviet Olympiad teams from 1958 to 1978, winning nine gold medals, one silver medal, and six individual gold medals. His overall performance in Olympiad play is impressive: he lost only one game out of 129 and scored 79.8 percent, the third all-time best performance.
Petrosian also made the Soviet team for the first eight European Team Championships (from 1957 to 1983). He won eight team gold medals and four board gold medals.
Legacy.
Petrosian was nicknamed "Iron Tigran" due to his almost impenetrable defensive playing style. Lev Polugaevsky said: "In those years, it was easier to win the Soviet Championship than a game against 'Iron Tigran.’’’
Petrosian made a colossal contribution to opening theory - many variations are named after him in different openings.
Armenia considers Petrosian a national hero. His picture is placed on the Armenian national currency.

Boris Spassky (born in 1937) was a Soviet, French, and Russian grandmaster, the tenth World Chess Champion (1969-1972).
Main chess achievements.
Spassky won the World Junior Chess Championship in 1955. One year later, he became the youngest grandmaster in the world.
In 1964, Spassky qualified for the newly created Candidates' Matches. In the 1965 cycle, he beat Paul Keres (quarterfinal), Efim Geller (semifinal), and Mikhail Tal (final), but his first world championship match against Petrosian ended with a loss (11.5-12.5).
Spassky again became a Challenger to the World Champion in the next cycle, and in 1969, he became the tenth World Chess Champion by beating Petrosian with a score of 12.5-10.5. His reign as world champion lasted for three years, as he lost to Fischer of the United States with a score of 12.5-8.5 in 1972. 
Spassky was a World Chess Championship candidate on seven occasions (1956, 1965, 1968, 1974, 1977, 1980, and 1985). In addition to his Candidates' wins in 1965 and 1968, Spassky reached the semi-final stage in 1974 and the final stage in 1977.
Spassky won the Soviet Chess Championship twice (1961 and 1973). Spassky won thirteen medals (eight team and five individual) in the Chess Olympiads for USSR. He won four gold medals (two team and two individual) in the European Team Championships (for USSR). Spassky played five times for the USSR in Student Olympiads, winning eight medals. He scored a fantastic 81.91 percent.
In 1992, Bobby Fischer, after a twenty-year hiatus from chess, re-emerged to arrange a "Revenge Match of the 20th century" against Spassky in Montenegro and Yugoslavia; this was a rematch of the 1972 World Championship. Spassky lost the match with a score of 12.5-17.5. However, Spassky earned $1.65 million for losing the match.
Legacy.
His contributions to opening theory are enormous.
The chess game between "Kronsteen" and "McAdams" in the early part of the James Bond movie From Russia With Love is based on a game played between Spassky and Bronstein in 1960 in which Spassky ("Kronsteen") was victorious.
The 1972 World Chess Championship match between Spassky and Fischer became the basis for a scenario of the biographical drama film Pawn Sacrifice.

Robert James Fischer (1943-2008) was an American chess player, the eleventh World Chess Champion (1972-1975). 
Main chess achievements.
Fischer became the youngest US Champion in his first US Championship (1957/1958). Later, Fischer played in seven more US Championships, winning all by at least a one-point margin.
Fischer finished fifth and fourth in his first two Candidates’ Tournaments (in 1958 and 1962, respectively). Fischer scored 8.5 points in the first ten games at the next Interzonal tournament. However, a conflict with the organizers about the schedule led to his withdrawal and elimination from the world championship cycle.
In the USSR vs. Rest of the World match in Belgrade 1970 ("the Match of the Century"), Fischer was the high scorer for his team, with a 3–1 score against Petrosian (two wins and two draws).
Fischer overwhelmed the field of the unofficial 1970 World Championship of Lightning Chess (5-minute games) with 19 points out of 22.
Fischer won the Interzonal in 1970 with a 3.5-point margin. In the 1971 Candidates’ matches, Fischer scored an impressive 6-0 against Mark Taimanov in the quarterfinal and Bent Larsen in the semifinal. Then he crushed Tigran Petrosian (6.5-2.5) in the final. These results gave Fischer a run of 20 consecutive wins against the world's top players, a winning streak topped only by Steinitz's 25 straight wins in 1873–1882.
Fischer won the World Championship match in Reykjavik in 1972 and became the eleventh World Chess Champion. In 1975, Fischer refused to play the next title match against Anatoly Karpov (due to a conflict about the match format) and forfeited his title. In 1992, Fischer beat Spassky in a "Revenge Match of the 20th Century" (held in Yugoslavia) with a prize fund of the match of 5 million USD.
Fischer represented the United States on the first board at four Chess Olympiads, winning two silver and one bronze medal.
Legacy.
In 1988, Fischer filed a U.S. patent for a new type of chess clock, giving each player a fixed period at the game's start and adding a small increment after each completed move. Today, all top tournaments and matches use this clock.
On June 19, 1996, in Buenos Aires, Argentina, Fischer announced and advocated a variant of chess called Fischer Random (later also known as Chess960). The goal of Fischer Random was to ensure that a game between two players is a contest between their understanding of chess rather than their abilities to prepare opening strategies or memorize opening lines. In 2019, FIDE organized the first World Fischer Random Chess Championship.
His book My 60 Memorable Games, published in 1969, is regarded as one of the great pieces of chess literature.
Many movies about Fischer’s life and chess career were released, such as Searching for Bobby Fischer, Me and Bobby Fischer, Bobby Fischer Live, and Pawn Sacrifice.
Many consider Fischer to be among the greatest and most famous chess players of all time.

Anatoly Karpov (born in 1951) is a Russian and former Soviet chess grandmaster, the twelfth World Chess Champion (1975-1985), and three-time FIDE World Champion (1993, 1996, 1998).
Main chess achievements.
Karpov won the World Junior Championship in 1969.
In 1973, Karpov scored 13.5 out of 17 and shared the 1–2 places (with Korchnoi) at the Leningrad 1973 Interzonal. After that, he won one-by-one Candidates’ matches against Lev Polugaevsky (quarterfinal, 5.5-2.5), Boris Spassky (semifinal, 7-4), and Viktor Korchnoi (final, 12.5-11.5) and qualified to play against Bobby Fischer for the World Championship in 1975. Unfortunately, the match with Fischer never happened because Fischer made demands that were not met. Karpov was declared the twelfth World Chess Champion.
After becoming the Champion, Karpov participated in nearly every major tournament for the next ten years. Karpov held the record for most consecutive tournament victories (9), which was shattered by Garry Kasparov (15).  He won two title matches against Viktor Korchnoi - 6-5 (with 21 draws) in 1978 and 6-2 (with ten draws) in 1981.
Karpov played five consecutive World Championship matches against Garry Kasparov from 1984 to 1990. Although Karpov never regained his title from Kasparov, their matches were all extremely close. They played a total of 144 games for the world championship, out of which 104 were draws, 21 wins for Kasparov, and 19 wins for Karpov.
In 1993, when Kasparov left FIDE and found the PCA, Karpov regained the FIDE World Champion title by defeating Jan Timman (12.5-8.5). Karpov defended this title twice - against Gata Kamsky in 1996 (10.5-7.5) and Viswanathan Anand in 1998 (3-3 in the main part of the match and 2-0 on the tie-break). In 1999, Karpov refused to play for the title.
In the top-level tournament Linares in 1994, Karpov scored 11 out of 13 with the highest performance rating (2985) until 2009, when Magnus Carlsen won the Pearl Spring chess tournament with a performance of 3002.
Karpov represented the Soviet Union at six Chess Olympiads, where the USSR won the team gold medal. Karpov twice was the World Chess champion as a member of the USSR team (1985, 1989).
Legacy.
The International Association of Chess Press awarded him nine Chess Oscars (an annual international award to the best chess player).
Karpov has authored or co-authored several books.
Karpov is considered to be one of the greatest players of all time. He was the highest-rated player in the world for over 100 months (a feat only bested by Garry Kasparov and Magnus Carlsen).

Garry Kasparov (born in 1963) is a Russian and former Soviet chess grandmaster, the thirteenth World Chess Champion (1985-2000).
Best chess achievements.
Kasparov won the Soviet Junior Championship (under 18) in Tbilisi in 1976, scoring 7 out of 9 points at age thirteen (!). He repeated the feat the following year, winning with a score of 8.5 out of 9 (!). He became the youngest master in the world by winning the Sokolsky Memorial in Minsk in 1978, outscoring the norm for 3.5 points (!). One year later, he finished two points ahead of the second player in the international tournament in Banja Luka (Yugoslavia) and got his first rating - 2595 (at the time, number 15 in the world). In 1980, he won the World Junior Chess Championship.
Kasparov won the Interzonal tournament of 1982 to qualify for the Candidates’ matches. One by one, he beat Alexander Beliavsky (quarterfinal, 6-3), Viktor Korchnoi (semifinal, 7-4), and Vasily Smyslov (final, 8.5-4.5) and gained the right to play the World Championship match. 
In the World Chess Championship match in 1984, Kasparov was down to 5-0 in a "first to six wins" match. By demonstrating extraordinary fighting skills, Kasparov managed to bring the score to 5-3. Then, the FIDE President stopped the match without a result and announced a new one. 
In the second match, Kasparov secured the thirteenth World Chess Champion title by scoring 13-11. After this, Kasparov and Karpov played three more World Championship matches. Kasparov consistently kept his title in all of them by scoring 12.5-11.5, 12-12, and 12.5-11.5.
In 1993, Nigel Short won the Candidates' matches to earn the title match against Kasparov. Neither the champion nor the challenger was comfortable with FIDE, resulting in a split in the championship titles. Kasparov and Short played under the auspices of the Professional Chess Association (PCA), created in 1986 by Kasparov. He won the match with a score of 12.5-7.5. Kasparov defended his title in 1995 in a match against Viswanathan Anand (10.5-7.5).
In 1996 and 1997, he played two matches with standard time control against IBM supercomputers. Kasparov won the first match (4-2). One year later, he lost 2.5-3.5.
The next PCA title match between Kasparov and Vladimir Kramnik occurred in London in 2000. Kramnik won 8.5-6.5 and became the new World Chess Champion (later recognized by FIDE).
When winning the Russian championship in 2004, he commented that it had been the last major title he had never won outright.
After winning the prestigious Linares tournament for the ninth time, Kasparov announced on 10 March 2005 that he would retire from regular competitive chess.
Kasparov played in a total of eight Chess Olympiads. He represented the Soviet Union four times and Russia four times. He won fourteen medals, including team gold medals, all eight times he competed.
Kasparov was ranked world No. 1 for a record 255 months overall for his career, the most in history.
He holds the record for most consecutive professional tournament victories, placing first or equal in 15 tournaments from 1981 to 1990.
In 1999, Kasparov reached an Elo rating of 2851 points. This record stood for over thirteen years. 
Legacy.
He was considered by many to be the greatest chess player in history. Kasparov won the Chess Oscar a record eleven times.
Kasparov has written many chess books. Practically all of them became chess bestsellers.
Garry Kasparov is affiliated with one of the first online chess platforms, Kasparov Chess.
He is a Chairman of the Kasparov Chess Foundation, which works with more than 3,500 schools throughout the United States and many more worldwide to promote the study of chess as a cognitive learning tool in curricular classes and after-school programs for elementary, middle, and high schools.

Vladimir Kramnik (born in 1975) is a Russian chess grandmaster, the fourteenth World Chess Champion (2000-2007).
Main chess achievements.
In January 1996, Kramnik became the youngest ever to reach world number one; this record would stand for 14 years until being broken by Magnus Carlsen in January 2010.
In March 2000, Kasparov announced he would play a match against Kramnik. Kramnik defeated his rival in London in 2000 with a score of 8.5–6.5 without losing a game and became the fourteenth World Chess Champion.
In 2004, Kramnik defended his title against challenger Peter Leko with a score of 7-7.
FIDE organized a reunification match between Kramnik and Veselin Topalov in 2006. Kramnik won it on the tie-break by a score of 2.5–1.5.
The next World Championship match was held in 2008 in Bonn, Germany, between Kramnik and Anand. Kramnik lost it by a score of 6.5-4.5.
Kramnik is the winner of the Chess World Cup 2013.
He won three team gold medals and three individual medals at Chess Olympiads as a member of the Russian team.
Legacy.
Kramnik was a player comfortable in any opening and, hence, any position. No opening is more closely associated with Kramnik than the Berlin Defense to the Ruy Lopez, thanks to his use of it to neutralize Kasparov’s use of the white pieces in 2000.

Viswanathan Anand (born in 1969) is an Indian grandmaster, the fifteenth World Chess Champion (2007-2013). 
Main chess achievements.
In 1985 (at age 15), Anand became the youngest Indian to achieve the title of International Master. At age 16, he became the national chess champion. 1987, he became the first Indian to win the World Junior Chess Championship. One year later, at age 18, he became India's first grandmaster. 
In 1993, Anand qualified for his first Candidates’ Tournament, winning his first match but narrowly losing his quarterfinal match to 1990 runner-up Anatoly Karpov.
In the 1995 PCA cycle, Anand won matches against Oleg Romanishin, Michael Adams, and Gata Kamsky and got the right to play in the PCA World Chess Championship match. He lost it against Kasparov with a score of 10.5–7.5 
In the 1998 FIDE cycle, FIDE granted Karpov, the reigning champion, direct seeding into the final against the seven-round single-elimination Candidates’ tournament winner. Anand won the tournament, defeating Adams in the final, and immediately faced Karpov for the championship. Despite this disadvantage for Anand, the regular match ended 3–3, leading to a rapid playoff in which Karpov won 2–0. Karpov thus remained the FIDE champion.
Two years later, Anand won the FIDE World Chess Championship 2000 (held in New Delhi, India, and Tehran, Iran).
In 2005, Anand finished in a tie for second place at the 2005 FIDE World Chess Championship, won by Topalov.
In 2007, Anand became the undisputed World Chess Champion, winning the World Chess Championship, an eight-player double round-robin tournament held in Mexico City. Anand convincingly defended the title against Kramnik in the 2008 World Chess Championship match held in 2008 in Bonn, Germany, with a score of 6.5-4.5. In 2010 and 2012, he defended his title in matches against Veselin Topalov and Boris Gelfand, respectively. In 2013, he lost the title to Magnus Carlsen. 
In 2014, Anand won the Candidates’ tournament and challenged the World Champion to a new match. Carlsen won it with a score of 6.5 to 4.5.
Anand won the 2003 and 2017 World Rapid Chess Championships.
Anand is the only player to have won the world chess championship in a tournament, match, knockout format, and rapid time control.
The great Indian won numerous prestigious tournaments and set many records. He is the first player to have won five titles in the Corus chess tournament (Wijk aan Zee, Netherlands). Anand has won the Mainz Chess Classic a record 11 times. He is the first player to have won each of the Big Three super tournaments at the time: Corus (1989, 1998, 2003, 2004, 2006), Linares (1998, 2007, 2008), and Dortmund (1996, 2000, 2004).
Anand has a stellar record at the annual Melody Amber Tournament (two separate Blindfold and Rapid Chess super tournaments played): he has five overall prizes (winning in 1994, 1997, 2003, 2005, and 2006) and has the most "rapid play" titles, winning nine times. He is also the only player to win the blind and rapid sections of the tournament in the same year (in 1997 and 2005).
Legacy.
Anand is considered one of the greatest players in history.
His results made chess one of the most popular sports in India. For Indian chess players, he is an undisputed King of Chess. He got the nickname “The Tiger of Madras”.

Magnus Carlsen (born in 1990) is a Norwegian chess grandmaster, the sixteenth World Chess Champion (2013-2023), five-time World Rapid Chess Champion (2014, 2015, 2019, 2022, 2023), and eight-time World Blitz Chess Champion (2009, 2014, 2017-2019, 2022-2024).
Main chess achievements.
At 13 years, four months, and 27 days of age, Carlsen became the youngest grandmaster in the world and the second-youngest grandmaster at the time.
At the end of 2005, he placed 10th at the FIDE World Cup to become the youngest player (15 years old) to qualify for a Candidates’ Tournament. In the first round of the 2007 tournament, he lost to the top-seed Levon Aronian. 
Carlsen won the 2009 Pearl Spring Chess Tournament with 8 out of 10 points, finishing 2.5 points ahead of the top-rated player in the world at the time, Veselin Topalov, and showing a tournament performance rating of fantastic 3002. It gave him a new rating of 2801, making him the fifth player in history to break 2800 and the youngest to do so until Firouzja in 2021.
In FIDE’s rating list for July 2011, Carlsen overtook Anand. Since then, he has remained the highest-rated player in the world.
Carlsen became the World Champion Challenger by winning the 2013 Candidates’ Tournament in London. He won the match against the reigning Champion, Viswanathan Anand, with a score of 6.5–3.5, becoming the new World Chess Champion.
In June 2014, the FIDE World Rapid & Blitz Chess Championships took place in Dubai. Carlsen scored 11 out of 15 points to win the World Rapid Championship, and two days later, he scored 17 ouf ot 21 points to take the World Blitz Championship and become triple world champion in all three time controls for the first time.
Carlsen defended the title against Anand in 2014 (6.5–4.5), Karjakin in 2016 (6-6 and 3-1 in a tie-break), Caruana in 2018 (6-6 and 3-0 in a tie-break), and Nepomniachtchi in 2021 (7.5-3.5).
After the latter match, Carlsen announced, "Unless Firouzja wins the Candidates Tournament, it is unlikely that I will play the next world championship match." Carlsen officially gave up the world champion title once Ding Liren defeated Nepomniachtchi in the 2023 FIDE World Championship.
In the spring of 2020, Carlsen announced the Magnus Carlsen Chess Tour, a series of five online tournaments with world-class players and a $1 million prize fund. The tour had four high-profile events (Carlsen won three of these events) and then the Finals for the four highest-scoring players. In the Finals, Carlsen narrowly defeated Nakamura.
In August 2023, Carlsen won the last big event, which he lacked in his fantastic collection - he became the winner of the World Cup 2023.
Legacy.
Magnus Carlsen is considered by many to be the greatest player of all time.
On the May 2014 FIDE rating list, Carlsen achieved his highest official rating of 2882. Earlier, during the Shamkir event, his live rating hit 2889.2 on April 21, 2014. Both are the highest ratings ever achieved in classical chess.
His unbeaten streak of 125 games in the classical time format is the longest at an elite level.

Ding Liren (born in 1992) is a Chinese chess grandmaster, the seventeenth World Chess Champion (2023-2024). 
Main chess achievements.
Ding won three Chinese national championships (2009, 2011, 2012). He went undefeated (!) in all of those events.
Ding has represented China at all four Chess Olympiads from 2012 to 2018, winning team gold medals in 2014 and 2018 and individual bronze and gold medals in 2014 and 2018, respectively. He also won team individual gold and silver at the World Team Championships 2015.
In July 2016, with a Blitz rating of 2875, he was the highest-rated Blitz player in the world.
In September 2017, he became the first Chinese to qualify for a Candidates’ Tournament. At the Candidates’ Tournament 2018, he placed 4th, the only player without a loss.
In September 2018, Ding became the first Chinese player to pass the 2800 Elo mark on the FIDE world rankings, and in November, he reached a rating of 2816, the joint-tenth highest rating in history.
Ding was undefeated in classical chess from August 2017 to November 2018, recording 29 victories and 71 draws. This 100-game unbeaten streak was the longest in top-level chess history until Carlsen surpassed it in 2019.
 In the 2017 and 2019 Chess World Cup, he became the first player in history to reach the finals twice in a row. In the first final, he lost to Levon Aronian; in the second, he lost to Teimour Radjabov.
Ding qualified for the Candidates’ Tournament 2022 by rating. He came in second, qualifying him for the World Chess Championship 2023 against Ian Nepomniachtchi, as Carlsen declined to defend his title. Ding won the 2023 World Championship match to become the first Chinese player ever to hold the title of World Chess Champion. After a bloody classical portion that ended tied 7-7 (3 wins for each player), he defeated Ian Nepomniachtchi in rapid tiebreaks, winning in the 4th game (2.5-1.5).
Ding was regarded as an underdog in the 2024 World Championship match against Gukesh Dommaraju, primarily because of his mental struggles throughout the year. Nevertheless, during the match, commentators acknowledged his remarkable fighting spirit. He lost the World Chess Championship title with a score of 6.5 to 7.5 after making one of the most tragic blunders in the championship's history during the final game.

Gukesh Dommaraju (born in 2006) is an Indian chess grandmaster and the reigning eighteenth World Chess Champion (from 2024).
Main chess achievements.
In 2018, he won the World Youth Chess Championship in the under-12 category and a record five gold medals (in individual rapid, blitz, and classical formats, and the team rapid and blitz competitions) in the under-12 events of the Asian Youth Chess Championship.
On 15 January 2019, Gukesh became the second-youngest grandmaster in the game's history, behind Sergey Karjakin, at the age of 12 years, 7 months, and 17 days.
In August 2022, Gukesh won the individual gold medal on the first board in the open event at the 44th Chess Olympiad in Chennai, scoring 9 out of 11. He was part of the India-2 team, which won the bronze medal.
In September 2022, Gukesh became the third-youngest to reach a FIDE rating over 2700 after Wei Yi and Alireza Firouzja. In August 2023, Gukesh became the youngest player ever to reach a rating of 2750.
In the Chess World Cup 2023 at Baku, he advanced to the quarterfinals, where he lost to Carlsen.
In the September 2023 rating list, Gukesh surpassed Viswanathan Anand as the top-ranked Indian player, marking the first time in 37 years that Anand was not the top-ranked Indian player.
In December 2023, Gukesh qualified for the 2024 Candidates Tournament. He was the third-youngest player to qualify for a Candidates tournament, behind Bobby Fischer and Magnus Carlsen. He became the youngest-ever winner of the Candidates tournament, scoring 9 out of 14 points.
In September 2024, Gukesh won the individual gold medal on the first board of the Chess Olympiad in Budapest with a score of 9 out of 10. His performance helped India to win their first-ever team gold medal at the Olympiad.
The 2024 World Chess Championship was held between Gukesh and Ding Liren in November–December 2024. Gukesh scored three wins against two wins for Ding and nine draws in the 14 games (7.5-6.5). The win made him the youngest undisputed World Chess Champion.

Paul Morphy (1837-1884) was an American chess player. Living before chess had a formal world championship, he is widely acknowledged to be the greatest chess master of his era.
Main chess achievements.
He won the First American Chess Congress in 1857, becoming the second official U.S. Champion. In 1858, he moved to England and France to challenge the leading players of Europe. He played formal and informal matches with most of the top English and French players and others, again winning all matches by large margins.
None were more important than his battles with German player Adolf Anderssen, as they were likely the two strongest players in the world at the time. The final score was 8-3 to Morphy. Morphy made numerous attempts at setting up a match with Howard Staunton, who was generally regarded as one of the world's strongest players at the time, but none ever came through.
He came back home in 1859 as a national hero. Baseball clubs and cigars were named after him. However, Morphy did not play serious chess again. At a time when chess was considered an amateur activity rather than a professional career, he turned his attention to his law practice.
Legacy.
Morphy was a revolutionary player, being the first to understand and demonstrate the value of long-term strategic development rather than premature attacks and traps. Nevertheless, he often won by spectacular piece sacrifices that continue to entertain and amaze admiring players.
 Paul Morphy played many famous games. One of them even has its own name - Opera Game. In 1858, Morphy played against two strong amateurs at an opera house in Paris: the German noble Karl II, Duke of Brunswick, and the French aristocrat Comte Isouard de Vauvenargues. Played as consultation games, he quickly developed his pieces, sacrificed a knight, a rook for a knight, and finally, his queen to checkmate his opponents as early as the 17th move.

 Adolf Anderssen (1818-1879) was a German chess master, one of the best players of his time, and the winner of the first international chess tournament (London 1851).
Main chess achievements.
The 1851 International Tournament in London was a knock-out event in which pairs of competitors played short matches, and Anderssen won it by beating Lionel Kieseritzky, Jozsef Szen, Staunton, and Marmaduke Wyvill – by margins of at least two games in every case. His prize was about £335, equivalent to about £240,000 ($370,200) in 2006. The win in this tournament established Anderssen as the world's leading chess player.
In late 1858, he was beaten 8–3 by the American champion Paul Morphy in a famous match in Paris, France. In 1860, Ignac Kolisch (a world-class player of the time) challenged Anderssen to a match, which was drawn. They played a rematch in 1861 that Anderssen won.
Anderssen won the London 1862 chess tournament, the first international round-robin tournament, with a score of twelve wins out of thirteen games. Morphy had retired from chess then, so Anderssen was again generally regarded as the world's leading active player.
In 1866, Anderssen lost a close match with the future first World Chess Champion Wilhelm Steinitz (six wins, eight losses, and no draws).
After this loss, Anderssen dedicated himself to chess, particularly studying both endgames and positional play. The result was that Anderssen, in his early fifties, was playing the finest chess of his career. As a result, Anderssen became the most successful tournament player in Europe, winning over half the events he entered, including the solid Baden-Baden 1870 chess tournament.
Legacy.
Anderssen played two famous games, both casual encounters, which he won by combinations that involved several sacrifices. In the first, as White against Lionel Kieseritzky in London in 1851, called the "Immortal Game," he sacrificed a bishop, both rooks, and finally, his queen. In the second, played in Berlin in 1852 as White against Jean Dufresne and now called the "Evergreen Game," the total sacrifice was more modest but still exceeded a queen and a minor piece.

Akiba Rubinstein (1880-1961) was a Polish chess player, one of the greatest players who was never a World Chess Champion.
Main chess achievements.
Between 1907 and 1912, Rubinstein established himself as one of the strongest players in the world. In 1907, he won the Carlsbad tournament and the All-Russian Masters' tournament and shared first at Saint Petersburg. In 1912, he had a record string of wins, finishing first in five consecutive major tournaments: San Sebastián, Postyen, Breslau, Warsaw, and Vilna, although none of these events included Lasker or Capablanca. Ratings from the Chessmetrics website support this conclusion, placing him as world No. 1 between mid-1912 and mid-1914.
In the pre-FIDE era, the reigning world champion handpicked his challenger, and Emanuel Lasker demanded a high sum of money that Rubinstein could not produce. A match with Lasker was finally arranged for October 1914, but it did not take place because of the outbreak of World War I. His playing after the war never regained the same consistency as he had pre-1914. 
Rubinstein was the leader of the Polish team that won the 1930 Chess Olympiad at Hamburg with a record of thirteen wins and four draws. He also won an Olympic silver at the 1931 Chess Olympiad, again leading the Polish team.
Legacy.
FIDE recognized his former strength when he was one of 27 players awarded the inaugural Grandmaster title in 1950.
He was exceptionally talented in the endgame, particularly in rook endings, where he broke new ground in knowledge. The endgame expert, Jeremy Silman, ranked him as one of the five best endgame players of all time and a master of rook endgames.
The Rubinstein Memorial tournament in his honor has been held annually since 1963 in Polanica Zdroj, with a glittering list of top-flight winners.

David Bronstein (1924-2006) was a Soviet chess player, one of the world's strongest players from the mid-1940s till the mid-1960s.
Best chess achievements.
Bronstein tied for first place in the Soviet Championships of 1948 and 1949. Bronstein's first major international tournament success occurred at the Saltsjobaden Interzonal of 1948, which he won. This win qualified him for the Candidates’ Tournament 1950 in Budapest, which ended with a tie between Bronstein and Isaac Boleslavsky. After that, Bronstein won the playoff match and became the winner of the first Candidates’ cycle.
Bronstein tied the 1951 World Championship match 12–12 with Mikhail Botvinnik, the reigning champion.
Bronstein finished tied for second place in the 1953 Candidates’ Tournament in Zurich. In the next cycle, he won the 1955 Gothenburg Interzonal. In the 1956 Candidates’ tournament in Amsterdam, Bronstein finished in a large tie for third through seventh places.
Bronstein played in 4 Chess Olympiads, winning eight medals (4 team gold, three individual gold, and one individual silver). He also won four medals in the European Team Championships.
Legacy.
David Bronstein wrote many chess books and articles. His book Zurich International Chess Tournament 1953 is considered one of the most outstanding chess books ever written.
In 1973, he introduced the idea of adding a small time increment for each move made, a variant of which has become very popular in recent years and is implemented on almost all digital chess clocks. 
Bronstein is considered one of the greatest players not to have won the World Championship. 

Paul Keres (1916-1975) was an Estonian chess grandmaster, one of the world's strongest players between the 1930s and 1960s.
Best chess achievements.
In 1938, he tied for first in the all-star AVRO tournament, held in various cities in the Netherlands and considered one of the strongest tournaments in history. He finished ahead of chess legends Capablanca, Alekhine, Euwe, Botvinnik (four World Champions from different years), Reshevsky, and Flohr. Keres won on a tiebreak by beating Fine 1.5-0.5. The winner of this tournament was expected to be the World Champion Challenger, but the outbreak of the Second World War brought negotiations with Alekhine to an end.
Keres participated in the 1948 World Championship tournament, arranged to determine the world champion after Alekhine died in 1946; he finished joint third. Keres finished second or equal second in four straight Candidates’ tournaments (1953, 1956, 1959, 1962). He was, therefore, occasionally nicknamed "Paul II."
He won the USSR Chess Championship thrice (1947, 1950, and 1951).
Keres represented the Soviet Union in seven consecutive Olympiads, winning seven successive team gold medals, five board gold medals, and one bronze board medal. His four straight board gold medals from 1954 to 1960 is an Olympiad record. Keres also appeared three times for the Soviet Union in the European Team Championships, winning team and individual gold medals on all three occasions.
Legacy.
His death occurred while returning to Estonia from a tournament in Vancouver, which he had won. Over 100,000 were in attendance at his funeral in Tallinn. A statue honoring him is located in Tallinn. A bronze statue of Keres was unveiled on his 100th birthday in his hometown, Narva. There is also a street in Nomme, a district of Tallinn, named after Keres.
The five kroons Estonian banknote bore his portrait.
An annual international chess tournament has been held in Tallinn every other year since 1969. Keres won this tournament in 1971 and 1975. Starting in 1976 after Keres' death, this tournament has been called the Paul Keres Memorial Tournament. There is also the annual Keres Memorial tournament in Vancouver and some chess clubs and festivals named after him.
In 2000, Keres was elected the Estonian Sportsman of the Century.
The World Chess Federation called 2016 "The Year of Paul Keres."

Viktor Korchnoi (1931-2016) was a Soviet (before 1976) and Swiss (after 1980) chess grandmaster, one of the world's strongest players from the early 1960s till the early 1990s.
Best chess achievements.
Korchnoi finished fifth in his first Candidates’ tournament in 1962.
He missed qualifying for the next world championship cycle, 1964–66, but he regained his form with overwhelming triumphs at Gyula 1965 with 14.5/15 and at Bucharest 1966 with 12.5/14.
A strong performance at the Interzonal, held in Sousse, Tunisia, took him through to the Candidates' matches in 1968. Korchnoi achieved the final stage, where Boris Spassky beat him with a score of 6.5-3.5.
In the 1975 World Championship cycle, Korchnoi tied for first place in the 1973 Leningrad Interzonal. In the Candidates' matches, he lost the Candidates' Final to Anatoly Karpov by a 12.5–11.5 score.
The next World Championship cycle (1976–78) gave Korchnoi his first title match against the reigning World Chess Champion. The World Championship Match of 1978 was held in Baguio, Philippines. There was enormous controversy off the board, but Karpov won the match by 6–5 with 21 draws. Three years later, Korchnoi again reached the World Championship Match and lost to Anatoly Karpov by a score of 11-7.
Korchnoi’s run at the World Championship cycles stretched from 1962 to 1991.
Korchnoi was the only player to play for each side in the series of two team matches, USSR vs. Rest of the World (in 1970, he lost to Lajos Portisch 1.5-2.5; in 1984 he scored 2.5 out of 4 against Lev Polugaevsky and Vladimir Tukmakov).
In 2006, Korchnoi won the 16th World Senior Chess Championship.
On the January 2007 FIDE rating list, Korchnoi was ranked number 85 in the world at age 75, the oldest player ever to be ranked in the FIDE top 100. The second-oldest player on the January 2007 list was Alexander Beliavsky, age 53.
Korchnoi became the oldest player to win a national championship, winning the 2009 Swiss championship at age 78. He won the national title again a few months after his 80th birthday in July 2011. Overall, Korchnoi won five Swiss championships.
Korchnoi was four times a USSR Chess Champion (1960, 1962, 1964/65, 1970), five times a member of Soviet teams that won the European championship, and six times a member of Soviet teams that won the Chess Olympiad.
Legacy.
Korchnoi defeated nine undisputed world champions (Botvinnik, Smyslov, Tal, Petrosian, Spassky, Fischer, Karpov, Kasparov, and Carlsen), a record he shares with Paul Keres and Alexander Beliavsky. Korchnoi’s name appears on a few opening lines.
Viktor Korchnoi is considered one of the strongest players never to have become the World Chess Champion.

Vasyl Ivanchuk (born in 1969) is a Ukrainian chess player, one of the world's strongest players from the late 1980s till the late 2010s.
Main chess achievements.
In 1987, Ivanchuk won the European Junior Chess Championship. In 1988, he won the New York Open ahead of many top grandmasters and tied for first place at the 1988 World Junior Championship. Then he won the Biel tournament, the Yerevan tournament, and the strongest 1989 Linares tournament. Ivanchuk tied for first place at the Manila Interzonal and qualified for the Candidates’ cycle for the first time.
Linares' victory in 1991 was a considerable achievement, which broke Kasparov's fantastic streak of tournament victories that had lasted since 1981. But after that, Ivahchuk lost his Candidates’ match against Artur Yusupov. 
Ivanchuk kept winning the super tournaments but, till 2002, had never been close to the World Championship match. In 2001 in Moscow, Ivanchuk reached the final of the 2002 FIDE World Championship, but in the final, another Ukrainian, Ruslan Ponomariov, defeated him, scoring 4.5-2.5. 
Ivanchuk became the European Champion of 2004. In 2005, he dominated the Capablanca Memorial tournament—he won this event eight times between 2005 and 2019. In 2007, he became the World Blitz Chess Champion. He climbed up to the world number-two ranking again this year.
Ivanchuk scored 8 out of 10 and won the 2008 M-Tel Masters with a 2959 performance rating. According to 2700chess.com, Ivanchuk reached his highest rating of 2791.5 on July 6, 2008—the 17th highest rating ever.
Ivanchuk won the 2016 World Rapid Championship.
Ivanchuk played in 14 Chess Olympiads, twice for the Soviet Union (1988 and 1990) and twelve times for Ukraine. He has won thirteen medals and been on four gold-medal-winning teams (USSR in 1988 and 1990, Ukraine in 2004 and 2010). He is a two-time World Champion in the team (once with the USSR and once with the Ukrainian team). He won 16 team and 12 individual medals in the biggest team competitions.
Legacy.
Many experts regard Ivanchuk as a chess genius.

Veselin Topalov (born in 1975) is a Bulgarian chess grandmaster, FIDE World Chess Champion (2005-2006).
Best chess achievements.
In 1996, he won his first major tournament by tying with Garry Kasparov at the Max Euwe Memorial (Amsterdam). He kept winning the elite tournaments till 2015. His honored list of the first (clear or shared) places includes Linares, Spain (2005 and 2010). Tata Steel Chess Tournament in Wijk aan Zee, Netherlands (2006 and 2007), Dortmund 2001, M-Tel Masters in Sofia, Bulgaria (2005-2007), Grand Slam Final 2008, Nanjing Pearl Spring 2008, Norway Chess 2015, and others.
His road to the World Chess Champion title wasn’t easy. In the 1999 FIDE World Championship knockout tournament, Topalov was eliminated by Kramnik in the fourth round. Michael Adams eliminated him in the 2000 FIDE World Championship knockout tournament quarterfinal. Then he lost to Alexei Shirov in the fourth round of the 2002 Championship. In the 2004 FIDE World Championship, Topalov lost to the future winner Rustam Kasimdzhanov in the semifinal.
In 2002, the Dortmund Sparkassen Chess Meeting acted as the Candidates’ Tournament to determine the challenger to the Kramnik title, which existed parallel to the FIDE title. Topalov lost in the Candidates’ final to Peter Leko.
In 2005, Topalov finally achieved his goal. Based on his rating, he was invited to the eight-player, double round-robin FIDE World Chess Championship in San Luis, Argentina. Scoring 6.5/7 in the first cycle, Topalov drew every game in the second cycle to win by 1.5 points to become the FIDE World Chess Champion.
FIDE organized the World Chess Championship match between Classical World Chess Champion Vladimir Kramnik and FIDE World Chess Champion Veselin Topalov in 2006. This match was to reunite the two World Chess Champion titles and produce an undisputed World Champion. Despite losing the match on a tie-break, Topalov remained the world's highest-rated player until January 2007.
The Challenger Match between Topalov and Gata Kamsky occurred in February 2009 in Sofia. Topalov won the match 4.5–2.5 and qualified for a new World Championship match in 2010 against Vishwanathan Anand. The match was very close, and the winner was determined in the last game, which Anand won with black pieces. The overall score was 6.5-5.5 in the Indian’s favor.
Topalov has competed on first board at nine Chess Olympiads for Bulgaria (1994-2000, 2008-2016), winning individual gold in 2014 and scoring the best overall performance in 1994.
Legacy.
His peak rating of 2816 placed him joint-tenth on the list of the  highest FIDE-rated players of all time.
Veselin Topalov has earned the nickname “The Destroyer” due to his aggressive and uncompromising playing style. In 2005, Topalov and his manager Silvio Danailov helped introduce the “Sofia Rule” to discourage short, pre-arranged draws in chess tournaments. This rule, first implemented at the M-Tel Masters Tournament in Sofia, Bulgaria, requires players to obtain permission from the arbiter before agreeing to a draw. The Sofia Rule has since been adopted in various international tournaments, helping to promote exciting and competitive games.

Peter Svidler (born in 1976) is a Russian chess grandmaster, the winner of the World Cup 2011, an 8-time Russian Champion, and a three-time World Chess960 Champion.
Main chess achievements.
In 1994, he became the World Chess Champion under 18 and then won his first Russian Championship, which gave him the Grandmaster title. Although he won the Russian Championship again in 1995 and 1997, his greatest tournament success of that period came at Tilburg Fontys (Netherlands) in 1997. Svidler defeated Garry Kasparov and tied him and Vladimir Kramnik for first place with 8 out of 11.
Svidler has a long list of solid results in the World Chess Championship cycles, though he has never played the title match. Participating in the FIDE 1998 World Championship tournament, he lost to Michael Adams in the fourth round. He came close to winning the FIDE World Championship 2002 when he fell to Ruslan Ponomariov in the semifinal. 
Svidler played in the 2005 FIDE World Championship, won by Veselin Topalov (tied for second place with Viswanathan Anand), and the 2007 World Championship, won by Anand (finished 5th).
He finished in a tie for third, scoring 8 out of 14, in the 2013 Candidates Tournament. Svidler played in two more Candidates’ Tournaments. He ended two points behind Anand in 2014 and 1.5 points behind Sergey Karjakin in 2016.
Svidler was very close to winning the second World Cup in 2015 when he failed in the final after winning the first two games against Sergey Karjakin.
Svidler is very successful in the team competitions. He played in 10 Chess Olympiads (from 1994 to 2014, skipping the 2012 event) and helped the Russian team win five gold and two silver medals. Individually, he won the bronze medal in 1996. He participated in eight European and five World Team championships for Russia, winning overall ten team (five gold, four silver, and one bronze) and seven individual medals (three gold, two silver, and two bronze). Svidler is a three-time winner of the European Club Cup with three different clubs (Ladya in 1997, Paris NAO in 2003, and Saint Petersburg in 2011). Additionally, he is a many-time winner of the Russian, French, and German club championships.
Peter Svidler is very strong in Fischer Random chess (also called chess960). He is a three-time World Chess960 Champion (2013-2015).
Legacy.
Playing in the era of Kasparov, Kramnik, Anand, and Carlsen is challenging, but Peter Svidler nonetheless became one of the top players of his generation. 
Peter Svidler is one of the most popular online chess commentators (both in English and Russian).

Judit Polgar (born in 1976) is a Hungarian chess grandmaster, the strongest female chess player of all time. 
Main chess achievements.
At 9, she won her first international tournament in New York. When Judit was 11, she defeated a Grandmaster in the tournament game. One year later, she became the youngest International Master at the time. In 1988 and 1990, she won the World Junior (for boys!) Championships (U-12 and U-14).
In 1988, at the age of 12, she played with her sisters for Hungary’s women’s team at the Women’s Chess Olympiad, and Judit scored 12.5 points out of 13 on board two, earning individual gold. The national team won gold as well. In 1990, Judit once again led the (same) national team to gold; she also won individual gold. It would be the last women’s-only tournament that she played in.
She entered the top 100 players in the world before turning 13 (!) years old. In 1991, Polgar won the Hungarian National Championship and received the grandmaster title at 15 years and five months. It beat Bobby Fischer’s record by a month.
Between 1990 and 2000, she won or tied for first place at 20 tournaments.
By 2004, Polgar advanced to the 8th place in the world rankings.
In September 2005, Polgar once again made history as she became the first woman to play in the final stages of the World Chess Championship qualification; she had previously participated in large, 100+ player knockout tournaments for the world championship, but this was a small 8-player invitational.
She won a bronze medal at the Men's European Championships in 2011. Polgar represented Hungary eight times as a member of the men's team at the Chess Olympiad, having won two silver medals.
In 2015, Polgar became the coach of the Hungarian men's chess team. Under her leadership, the team won a bronze medal at the European Championships in Iceland.
Legacy.
Polgar became the top-ranked female player in the world in 1989 (at 12 years old), which lasted into her retirement more than 26 years later (a Guinness World Record). Polgar is the best woman ever to play chess. 
In 2015, she received the Grand Cross of the Order of Saint Stephen of Hungary, Hungary’s highest decoration and originally an order of knighthood. 
Polgar continues to be active in the chess community as a commentator, writer, lecturer, and more. She focuses on her family, the international promotion of the sport, book writing, and the Judit Polgar Chess Foundation, which was established in 2012.

Levon Aronian (born in 1982) is an Armenian-American grandmaster, one of the world's strongest players from the late 1990s until the present time.
Main chess achievements.
In 2001, Aronian placed second in both the World Junior Championship and the Armenian Chess Championship. In 2002, he won both titles. From 2003 to 2005, he strung together three top-four finishes in the annual European Individual Chess Championships.
Aronian won the Chess World Cup 2005 without losing a single game. In 2017, he won the Chess World Cup again and became the only player to win this trophy twice.
In 2006, Aronian won his first elite tournament - Linares. The list of his major wins includes  the Tata Steel Chess Tournament in Wijk aan Zee, Netherlands (2007, 2008, 2012, 2014), Tal Memorial in Moscow, Russia (2010, 2011), FIDE Grand Prix stages (Sochi 2008, Nalchik 2009, Palma de Mallorca 2017), Grand Slam Final 2009 and 2014, Alekhine Memorial in Paris and Saint Petersburg 2013, Sinquefield (2015, 2018), Altibox in Norway (2017), Superbet Chess Classic in Bucharest, Romania (2022), WR Chess Masters in Dusseldorf, Germany (2023) and others.
Unfortunately, Aronian was always unsuccessful in the tournaments for the World Championship cycle. In the 2007 World Championship Tournament, he placed seventh out of eight players. Despite being a favorite in the Candidates’ Tournament 2012, he lost to Grischuk in first-round tiebreakers. In the Candidates’ Tournament 2014, Aronian tied 6–7 places (out of 8); two years later, he tied 4–7 places (out of 8); two more years later, he finished the last.
At the same time, Aronian was highly successful in the team competitions. At the 37th Chess Olympiad 2006, held in Turin, the Armenian national team amazingly became the Champions. Two years later, in Dresden, Armenia captured a second consecutive gold medal, led once again by Aronian. In 2012, in Istanbul, Aronian's team seized a third Olympic champion title. Aronian had the best performance rating (2849) of all players on board one. Moreover, in 2011, the Armenian team, led by Levon Aronian, won the World Team Chess Championship in Ningbo, China 2011. This team record remains unprecedented since Armenia has never been ranked in the top 3 by rating.
Aronian is an extremely strong player with short-time controls. He won the World Rapid Chess Championship in 2009. One year later, he won the World Blitz Championship.
Considering his creative style, Aronian was always successful in Fischer Random Chess. In 2003, he earned a match against then-world champion Peter Svidler but lost. Two years later, Aronian won the qualification tournament again, earned a rematch, and won the Chess960 World Champion title. He defended the title in 2007 against Viswanathan Anand and lost it in 2009 to Hikaru Nakamura.
Legacy.
In the first months of 2014, Aronian achieved live (2835) and real (2830) record ratings, making him the fourth-highest-rated player in history. Aronian was no worse than the world's number-three player for nearly four years—from November 2010 to September 2014. 

Hikaru Nakamura (born in 1987) is an American grandmaster, one of the world's strongest players from the early 2010s till the present time, and one of the most successful chess streamers, and YouTubers.
Best chess achievements.
Nakamura won the 2005 U.S. Chess Championship and became the youngest national champion since Fischer. After that, he won four more titles (in 2009, 2012, 2015, and 2019).
Nakamura’s performance at the World Team Chess Championship of 2010 won him the individual gold medal for board one and led the U.S. to a second-place finish behind Russia. He has represented the United States at five Chess Olympiads, securing a gold medal and two bronze medals.
Nakamura won the Tata Steel tournament in Wijk aan Zee 2011, finishing ahead of four top-ranked players in the world. Garry Kasparov called Nakamura's victory the best by an American in more than 100 years. 
Nakamura is exceptionally skilled at rapid, blitz, and bullet chess. In May 2014, when FIDE began publishing official rapid and blitz chess ratings, Nakamura ranked No. 1 in the world on both lists; he has consistently remained at or near the No. 1 rank ever since.
Nakamura became the Fischer Random World Champion in 2022.
In 2018, Nakamura began streaming on the Twitch platform under GMHikaru. He plays speed chess games, variously against grandmasters, other streamers, and viewers who pay to subscribe to his channel. On February 14, 2021, Nakamura reached a milestone of one million followers on his Twitch channel, GMHikaru. Nakamura also has a YouTube channel, which has over 2M subscribers. 
Nakamura has stated that he prioritizes his streaming career over his chess-playing career. Despite this, in June 2023, he returned to second place in the world rankings (behind Magnus Carlsen).
Legacy.
Hikaru Nakamura is undoubtedly the most popular chess player in the U.S. since Fischer's times. 
Nakamura appeared as himself in season 5, episode 2 of the Showtime series Billions, which premiered May 10, 2020.
His highest rating of 2816 is the tenth-highest in history.

Fabiano Caruana (born in 1992) is an Italian and American chess grandmaster, one of the world's strongest players from the early 2010s to the present.
Main chess achievements.
Caruana is a four-time Italian Champion  (2007, 2008, 2010, and 2011) and a four-time U.S. Champion (2016, 2022-2024). Caruana won a silver medal in the World Rapid Championships 2014 in Dubai.
He won the Sinquefield Cup 2014 with a performance of 3098 - the highest in history.
Having won the FIDE Grand Prix 2014–15, Caruana qualified for the Candidates’ Tournament 2016, where he was runner-up to Sergey Karjakin. Two years later, he won the Candidates’ Tournament and challenged the reigning World Chess Champion for the match. Caruana lost to Magnus Carlsen in the match, held in New York City in 2018, in the rapid tiebreaks after drawing all twelve classical games.
Caruana shared third-fourth place in the next Candidates’ tournament (in 2020).
By finishing second at the 2021 FIDE Grand Swiss, he qualified for his fourth straight Candidates’ Tournament. Despite a strong start, Caruana eventually finished in fifth place. As one of the main favorites in the 2024 Candidates’ Tournament, Caruana scored half a point less than the winner, Gukesh Dommaraju.
He represented the United States on board one at the 42nd Chess Olympiad, held in Baku in 2016, winning team gold and individual bronze. He led the United States to a silver medal in the Olympiad in Batumi in 2018.
In addition to the above, Caruana has a long list of elite tournament wins: Dortmund (2012, 2014, 2015), Grand Slam Masters Final (2012), Zurich Chess Challenge (2013), Romgaz Kings (2013), Gashimov Memorial (2016), London Chess Classic (2017), Grenke Chess Classic (2018), Altibox Norway (2018), Sinquefield Cup (2018), Tata Steel (2020), Superbet Chess Classic (2023).
Legacy.
According to 2700chess.com on October 8, 2014, Caruana's live rating of 2851 is the third-highest in history (after Magnus Carlsen and Garry Kasparov).
He had a breakout stint as a commentator for the 2021 FIDE World Championship and recently began a popular podcast with his longtime second, Cristian Chirila.

Ian Nepomniachtchi (born in 1990) is a Russian chess grandmaster, one of the world's strongest players from the early 2010s until the present time. 
Best chess achievements.
In 2010, Nepomniachtchi won the European Individual Championship. Later the same year, he won the Russian Chess Championship. In October 2013, he tied for first with Peter Svidler in the Russian Championship Superfinal, finishing second on tiebreak.
In the FIDE Grand Prix 2019 series, Nepomniachtchi won two out of three tournaments in which he played. He finished second in the overall standings, qualifying him for the 2020 Candidates’ Tournament. In December 2020, he again won the Russian championship.
A win in the 2020/2021 Candidates’ tournament qualified Nepomniachtchi to challenge Magnus Carlsen in a match for the World Chess Championship in November–December 2021. Carlsen retained his title, winning 7.5–3.5.
Nepomniachtchi qualified for the 2022 Candidates’ tournament as the World Championship runner-up and took an early lead. He won by getting the highest score of 9.5 out of 14 in any Candidates tournament since the modern format was introduced in 2013.
In his second FIDE World Chess Championship 2023, Nepomniachtchi had a tied score of 7–7 with Ding Liren in the classical portion of the match but ended up losing 2.5–1.5 in the tiebreak playoff.
Nepomniachtchi won numerous prestigious chess tournaments: Aeroflot Open (2006 and 2015), Danzhou (2016), Tal Memorial (2016), London Chess Classic (2017), Dortmund (2018), Sinquefield Cup 2022 (Saint Louis), WR Chess Masters 2023 (Dusseldorf) and others.
Nepomniachtchi is exceptionally strong in fast time controls; however, he has struggled for a long time to secure the World Blitz or Rapid title. He finished second at the World Blitz Championship in 2014 and earned three medals in the World Rapid Championships: a bronze in 2017 and 2024 and a silver in 2021. Finally, on December 31, 2024, he became a co-winner (with Magnus Carlsen) of the World Blitz Chess Championship.
Legacy.
Together with Vasily Smyslov, Nepomniachtchi is the only one who managed to win two Candidates' tournaments.
