# Paul Morphy

Paul Morphy (1837-1884) was an American chess player. Living before chess had a formal world championship, he is widely acknowledged to be the greatest chess master of his era.

## Main chess achievements

He won the First American Chess Congress in 1857, becoming the second official U.S. Champion. In 1858, he moved to England and France to challenge the leading players of Europe. He played formal and informal matches with most of the top English and French players and others, again winning all matches by large margins.

None were more important than his battles with German player Adolf Anderssen, as they were likely the two strongest players in the world at the time. The final score was 8-3 to Morphy. Morphy made numerous attempts at setting up a match with Howard Staunton, who was generally regarded as one of the world's strongest players at the time, but none ever came through.

He came back home in 1859 as a national hero. Baseball clubs and cigars were named after him. However, Morphy did not play serious chess again. At a time when chess was considered an amateur activity rather than a professional career, he turned his attention to his law practice.

## Legacy

Morphy was a revolutionary player, being the first to understand and demonstrate the value of long-term strategic development rather than premature attacks and traps. Nevertheless, he often won by spectacular piece sacrifices that continue to entertain and amaze admiring players.

Paul Morphy played many famous games. One of them even has its own name - Opera Game. In 1858, Morphy played against two strong amateurs at an opera house in Paris: the German noble Karl II, Duke of Brunswick, and the French aristocrat Comte Isouard de Vauvenargues. Played as consultation games, he quickly developed his pieces, sacrificed a knight, a rook for a knight, and finally, his queen to checkmate his opponents as early as the 17th move.
