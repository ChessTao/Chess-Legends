# Adolf Anderssen

Adolf Anderssen (1818-1879) was a German chess master, one of the best players of his time, and the winner of the first international chess tournament (London 1851).

## Main chess achievements

The 1851 International Tournament in London was a knock-out event in which pairs of competitors played short matches, and Anderssen won it by beating Lionel Kieseritzky, Jozsef Szen, Staunton, and Marmaduke Wyvill – by margins of at least two games in every case. His prize was about £335, equivalent to about £240,000 ($370,200) in 2006. The win in this tournament established Anderssen as the world's leading chess player.

In late 1858, he was beaten 8–3 by the American champion Paul Morphy in a famous match in Paris, France. In 1860, Ignac Kolisch (a world-class player of the time) challenged Anderssen to a match, which was drawn. They played a rematch in 1861 that Anderssen won.

Anderssen won the London 1862 chess tournament, the first international round-robin tournament, with a score of twelve wins out of thirteen games. Morphy had retired from chess then, so Anderssen was again generally regarded as the world's leading active player.

In 1866, Anderssen lost a close match with the future first World Chess Champion Wilhelm Steinitz (six wins, eight losses, and no draws).

After this loss, Anderssen dedicated himself to chess, particularly studying both endgames and positional play. The result was that Anderssen, in his early fifties, was playing the finest chess of his career. As a result, Anderssen became the most successful tournament player in Europe, winning over half the events he entered, including the solid Baden-Baden 1870 chess tournament.

## Legacy

Anderssen played two famous games, both casual encounters, which he won by combinations that involved several sacrifices. In the first, as White against Lionel Kieseritzky in London in 1851, called the "Immortal Game," he sacrificed a bishop, both rooks, and finally, his queen. In the second, played in Berlin in 1852 as White against Jean Dufresne and now called the "Evergreen Game," the total sacrifice was more modest but still exceeded a queen and a minor piece.
